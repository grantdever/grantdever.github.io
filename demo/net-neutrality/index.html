The Internet Didn’t End. New Competitors Showed Up Instead.

In the years since the FCC repealed its 2015 net neutrality rules, American broadband has become faster, more widely adopted and more competitive. According to the FCC’s own measurements, cable download speeds rose from 150.8 Mbps in 2018 to 401.2 Mbps in 2022, while the share of households with a broadband subscription of any type grew from 85.1% in 2018 to 93.2% in 2024.

Grant Dever · October 2026

The new connections went largely to challengers rather than to cable. Fiber connections nearly tripled to 35.99 million by June 2025, and fiber and fixed wireless together grew from 14% of home connections in December 2017 to 37% in June 2025, while cable connections plateaued near 74.5 million in 2021–22 and have since fallen to 69.25 million. In 2025 alone, T-Mobile, Verizon and AT&T added 3.92 million fixed-wireless customers as Comcast and Charter lost 1.12 million. Between June 2023 and June 2025, the share of households with three or more providers offering 100/20 Mbps service rose from 27.8% to 43.3%.

Consumers did not end up paying more for less. Cable revenue per customer rose 33–35% in nominal dollars, slightly faster than inflation, but the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ quality-adjusted price index for internet service fell 14.8% after inflation, and Comcast’s and Charter’s real revenue per customer has slipped since 2021. Our search of the FCC’s 2024 order and the Free Press violations list found no paid fast lane, no pay-for-play deal and no major ISP blocking lawful websites.

Investment is the weakest part of the record. After inflation, broadband capital spending from 2018 to 2025 averaged within 1% of its 2017 level, and 2025 alone was 8.3% below it, so the national totals do not show the increase the FCC predicted. Fiber builders, meanwhile, set records for new homes passed in every year from 2022 (according to the Fiber Broadband Association).
